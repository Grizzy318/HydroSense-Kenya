Executive Summary
HydroSense-Kenya is a comprehensive scientific computing system developed to address water-use efficiency
challenges in Kenyan agricultural contexts. The project implements a complete workflow from data acquisition
through numerical modeling, simulation, optimization, and decision support for smart irrigation management.
Leveraging Python's scientific computing ecosystem, the system processes real-world sensor data, implements core
numerical methods from scratch, simulates soil moisture dynamics under uncertainty, and generates optimized
irrigation recommendations. The project follows a six-level incremental design, demonstrating proficiency in
computational thinking, numerical methods, data science, simulation, and responsible AI-assisted programming. 
1. Introduction and Problem Framing
1.1 Scientific Problem Statement
Water scarcity poses a critical challenge to agricultural productivity in Kenya, particularly for smallholder farmers
and agricultural innovation hubs. Climate variability, inefficient irrigation practices, and limited access to real-time
decision support tools exacerbate this challenge, leading to crop stress, reduced yields, and water wastage. The
central scientific question addressed by HydroSense-Kenya is:

1.2 Computational Problem Translation
We translated this agricultural challenge into a computational problem requiring:
Data Processing: Cleaning and analyzing weather, soil moisture, and crop parameter datasets.
Mathematical Modeling: Implementing the water balance equation with evapotranspiration estimation.
Numerical Methods: Root finding for irrigation amount calculation, differentiation for moisture change rates,

Simulation: Modeling soil moisture dynamics using differential equations with uncertainty quantification.
Optimization: Minimizing irrigation water while respecting moisture constraints.
Visualization: Creating interpretable scientific figures for decision support.
1.3 Project Context and Significance
The project was developed for a university demonstration farm context, representing a typical Kenyan agricultural
innovation  scenario.  The  synthetic  datasets  provided  realistic  challenges  including  missing  values,  sensor
anomalies, and outliers, mirroring real-world data quality issues. This approach ensured that our solution would be
applicable to actual smart irrigation systems being deployed across Kenya's agricultural sector. 
1.4 Assumptions and Limitations
Key Assumptions:
Daily time steps are sufficient for modeling soil moisture dynamics.
The simplified evapotranspiration formula (ET = max(0, 0.12 × T + 0.35 × W + 2.4 × Solar - 0.025 × H))
adequately represents crop water loss for teaching purposes.
Soil moisture behaves as a single-layer bucket model.
Irrigation can be applied at the beginning of each day.
Weather patterns follow the observed historical distributions.
Limitations:
Does not account for crop growth stage variations in water requirements.
Simplified drainage model without considering soil texture heterogeneity.
No groundwater table interaction modeling.

2. Mathematical Model and Numerical Methods
2.1 Core Water Balance Model
The foundation of our system is the discrete water balance equation:
S(t+1) = S(t) + R(t) + I(t) - ET(t) - D(t)
Where:
S(t) = Soil water storage/moisture at time t
R(t) = Rainfall contribution at time t
I(t) = Irrigation applied at time t
ET(t) = Evapotranspiration (crop water loss)
D(t) = Drainage beyond field capacity
2.2 Evapotranspiration Estimation
We implemented the simplified empirical formula:
ET = max(0, 0.12 × T + 0.35 × W + 2.4 × Solar - 0.025 × H)
Where T is Temperature (°C), W is Wind speed (m/s), Solar is Solar intensity index, and H is Humidity (%).
2.3 Numerical Methods Implemented
2.3.1 Root Finding Methods
We implemented three root-finding algorithms to determine the irrigation amount needed to reach target soil
moisture:
Bisection Method: Robust but slow, guaranteed convergence.
Newton-Raphson Method: Fast convergence but requires derivative calculation.
Secant Method: Derivative-free alternative with super-linear convergence.
Performance comparison showed Newton-Raphson converged in 4-6 iterations compared to 12-15 for bisection,
though it required careful handling near inflection points.

2.3.2 Numerical Differentiation
Forward, backward, and central difference approximations were implemented to estimate rates of soil moisture
change:
Forward: f'(x) ≈ (f(x+h) - f(x)) / h
Backward: f'(x) ≈ (f(x) - f(x-h)) / h
Central: f'(x) ≈ (f(x+h) - f(x-h)) / (2h)
Central difference provided superior accuracy (O(h²) vs O(h)) and was used for daily moisture change rates.
2.3.3 Numerical Integration
Cumulative water deficits were estimated using:
Trapezoidal Rule: Piecewise linear approximation.
Simpson's Rule: Piecewise quadratic approximation.
Simpson's method showed lower error for smooth moisture curves, with relative errors below 0.5% compared to
analytical solutions.
2.3.4 Linear Systems for Multi-Zone Allocation
A three-zone water allocation problem was solved as  Ax = b using Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting,
enabling simultaneous optimization of water distribution across tomato, kale, and maize zones.
2.4 Differential Equation Solvers
Continuous soil moisture dynamics were modeled using:
Euler Method: Simple explicit method with O(h) accuracy.
Runge-Kutta Method: Fourth-order method with O(h⁴) accuracy.
The RK4 method demonstrated superior stability for the stiff moisture balance equations, maintaining physical
plausibility (non-negative moisture) even with larger time steps.

Our cleaning decisions included:
Missing Values (Weather): Linear interpolation for March 8 rainfall using neighboring day averages.
Missing Values (Soil): Forward-fill for Zone_B soil moisture on March 6; March 21 value was retained as
sensor "CHECK" and validated against weather patterns.
Outlier Detection and Treatment:
Tank Level (9900L): Identified as sensor error on March 14; replaced with moving average of surrounding
days (≈4246L).
Soil Moisture (8.5%): March 25 Zone_B anomalous low value; cross-validated with weather and other
zones, flagged as outlier but retained for analysis after considering potential sensor drift.
Sensor Status: "CHECK" flag on March 21 maintained in dataset; corresponding moisture value used with
confidence weighting.
Unit Consistency: All variables verified for consistent units throughout.

4. Simulation and Optimization Results
4.1 Soil Moisture Simulation
Euler Method Results (30-day simulation): Initial moisture: 33.2% (Zone_A), Final moisture: 24.2%, Minimum
moisture: 22.6% on March 28. It took 27 days to reach minimum thresholds, prompting 8 days of mandatory
irrigation events to sustain the crop.
Runge-Kutta Comparison: RK4 produced significantly smoother moisture trajectories (less oscillatory) with a
maximum divergence between methods of 2.3% moisture. Euler exhibited numerical instability during rapid
rainfall peaks; RK4 is highly recommended for production deployment.
4.2 Monte Carlo Uncertainty Analysis
We performed 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations with uncertain rainfall inputs:
Probability of water shortage (moisture < 22%): 34%
Probability of over-irrigation (>40% moisture): 12%
Expected irrigation demand: 142,500 liters / 30 days
Worst-case demand (95th percentile): 231,000 liters / 30 days
Parameter Sensitivity: Rainfall (68% of variance), Evapotranspiration (22% of variance).

4.3 Optimized Irrigation Schedule
The optimization algorithm (gradient descent) minimized water use subject to moisture constraints:
Total water applied: 124,800 liters (a 12% absolute reduction from baseline).
Irrigation events: 12 of 30 days. Max irrigation occurred in Zone_C (maize).
Pumping time: Reduced by 18% compared to the fixed schedule.
Trade-off Analysis: More frequent, smaller applications (daily/near-daily) performed significantly better than
infrequent large water dumps, preventing excessive drainage and maintaining ideal soil physics.
5. AI-Assisted Programming and Validation
We utilized AI-assisted tools for automated test generation, docstring standardization, and visualization advice. All
outputs were verified using strict validation logic: 
Test Case Generation: Generated 5 pytest cases for the bisection method. Adjusted convergence tolerances
manually from 1e-6 to 1e-5 and successfully cross-validated with scipy.optimize.bisect.
Code Optimization: NumPy vectorization was used to eliminate slow loops inside the ET calculation step,
accelerating execution by 3.2x.
6. Code Architecture and Implementation
The system follows a clean modular architecture separated into data, notebooks, source code (src/), and unit tests
(tests/). Over 65 unit and integration tests are executed via  pytest, achieving an excellent  94% code
coverage metric.
Floating Point Error Note
Numerical safety measures were put in place to handle floating point limitations (such as 0.1 + 0.2 !=
0.3). All logical equality evaluations utilize tolerance-based boundaries (1e-5) rather than direct operators
to eliminate error propagation risk. 
7. Results and Discussion
The results successfully validate precision irrigation engineering. The optimized schedule maintained all crop zones
within their ideal targeted moisture ranges while reducing water consumption by 12%. This translates into a savings
of approximately 17,000 liters over a 30-day window for this specific demonstration asset. Rainfall uncertainty
remains the single largest risk element, highlighting that real-time predictive adjustments are superior to any static
blueprint schedule.
